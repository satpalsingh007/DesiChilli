Awarapan 2 released on August 14, nearly two decades after the 2007 original built its cult following, with Nitin Kakkar taking over direction from Mohit Suri. The reviews since have followed an unusually consistent shape: critics disagree on almost everything about the film except one thing — Emraan Hashmi is the reason it works at all.

## Why are the reviews split on Awarapan 2?

Read enough reviews and a pattern emerges. Several critics flagged the same problems: underwritten supporting characters, a weak antagonist, and a third act that loses the grip the film builds early on. One review went as far as calling the casting the film's biggest weakness. And yet almost every one of those same reviews carves out a specific exception for Hashmi's performance, treating it as the thing single-handedly keeping the film watchable through its rougher stretches.

That's not a small gap. It's the difference between a film critics are calling structurally flawed and a film theatre audiences are responding to enthusiastically anyway, nostalgia and lead-performance goodwill doing the work the script apparently couldn't.

## What does Awarapan 2 say about Bollywood's sequel economy?

This isn't really a story about one film — it's a pattern Bollywood keeps proving works. A beloved character, a genuinely committed lead performance, and two decades of built-up nostalgia can carry a sequel through script problems that would sink an original film outright. Awarapan 2 didn't need a tight screenplay to open well [opposite Batwara 1947 on Independence Day weekend](/posts/studios-keep-clashing-releases-everyone-loses); it needed Hashmi to show up and mean it, and by most accounts, he did. Toxic, by contrast, [spent five months rescheduling its way to a weekend of its own](/posts/toxic-avoided-box-office-clash-three-times-worked) — two very different ways to protect an opening number.

That's not necessarily a criticism of the film itself — a strong lead performance is a real achievement, not a consolation prize. But it's worth being honest about what's actually driving ticket sales when a sequel gets called "a mass-appealing entertainer" in the same reviews that call its writing thin. Audiences aren't rewarding a well-told story here. They're rewarding a well-performed one, and Bollywood's sequel slate increasingly seems to know the difference — and bet on the second one every time. Which makes it the near-mirror image of [Hanuman Ansh, a film with no star and no budget that climbed anyway](/posts/hanuman-ansh-proves-big-budgets-arent-the-point).
