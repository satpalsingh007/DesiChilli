Toxic's opening day was a genuinely massive number — trade reports put it around ₹118 crore in India alone, among the biggest single-day openings any Hindi-language release has managed this year. By its first Monday, that had collapsed to somewhere between ₹3.5 and ₹7 crore, a drop of roughly 94%. Trade press is already comparing the fall to Thugs of Hindostan, a film that's become industry shorthand for a huge opening with nothing behind it to sustain a second week.

## Why does the Monday drop change the earlier read?

[We wrote here recently](/posts/toxic-avoided-box-office-clash-three-times-worked) about how Toxic's repeated release-date delays — dodging a head-on clash with Dhurandhar 2 across three separate reschedules — looked like a smart, patient strategy once the film finally opened huge. That read now needs an honest update. Avoiding a rival got Toxic an uncontested opening weekend. It didn't protect the film from what happens after an opening weekend, which is where this story has actually gone sideways.

## What does a 94% Monday crash usually mean?

A 94% single-day drop isn't ordinary front-loading — most big openers lose ground on a Monday, but not almost all of it. That scale of fall usually points to one specific thing: an opening built on hype, star power, and a saturation release across roughly 12,000 screens, running into a story that isn't giving audiences a reason to tell anyone else to go see it. Reviews for Toxic have been mixed at best on substance even where they credit its visual ambition, and a ₹500–800 crore budget only guarantees a big first weekend — it can't manufacture word of mouth after that.

## How does Toxic compare to Hanuman Ansh right now?

It's hard not to put this next to [Hanuman Ansh's run](/posts/hanuman-ansh-proves-big-budgets-arent-the-point) this same month — a ₹1.8 crore devotional drama that opened to barely ₹10 lakh and has spent three weeks climbing as word of mouth pulled more screens and more audiences toward it. Toxic and Hanuman Ansh are moving in exactly opposite directions on the same chart right now, for exactly the reason you'd expect: one film needed an audience to show up because of a marketing campaign, and the other needed an audience to show up because the last audience told them to. Only one of those is a strategy a studio can actually repeat. [Mirzapur: The Movie](/posts/mirzapur-bets-fans-will-pay-for-what-was-free), opening the same week, is about to run its own version of that test.
